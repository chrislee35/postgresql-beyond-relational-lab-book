Getting a Business License in Portsmith

Anyone opening a business within city limits needs a business license from
the Planning & Zoning department before serving a single customer. The
process starts online through the city's permitting portal, where you
choose a business category — retail, restaurant, service, accommodation,
or entertainment — since the category determines which inspections and
follow-up permits apply. A restaurant, for instance, also triggers a
health department inspection and a fire marshal walkthrough before the
license is issued, while a small retail shop typically only needs the
base business license and a certificate of occupancy for the space.

Most applications are processed within two to three weeks, though anything
involving food service or alcohol takes longer because those categories
require a scheduled in-person inspection rather than a paperwork review.
Applicants should not sign a lease assuming a fast turnaround; the city
recommends applying for the license before finalizing a location wherever
possible, since some spaces are zoned in ways that don't permit every
business category outright.

License fees are set by the current fee schedule maintained by the
finance department and reviewed periodically by the city council — the
schedule varies by category and is not fixed year to year, so applicants
should always confirm the current fee at the time of application rather
than relying on a figure they heard from another business owner. Fees are
generally lower for small, owner-operated shops and higher for categories
that require more city oversight, such as late-night entertainment venues.

Renewals happen annually and are due on the anniversary of the original
license date. A business that lets its license lapse for more than ninety
days has to reapply from scratch rather than simply renewing, which means
a new round of inspections for categories that require them. The city
sends a renewal reminder by mail and email sixty days before expiration,
but responsibility for renewing on time rests with the business owner, not
the city.

Home-based businesses — a hairdresser working out of a spare room, a
bookkeeper seeing clients from home — still need a business license, but
qualify for a simplified home occupation permit instead of a full
commercial inspection, provided the business doesn't involve retail foot
traffic, on-site employees other than household members, or exterior
signage visible from the street. Anyone unsure whether their home-based
idea qualifies should contact Planning & Zoning directly rather than
guessing, since operating without the correct permit can result in fines
even if the business itself is entirely legal.

Businesses that want to change their registered category — a retail shop
adding a small cafe counter, for example — need to file a category
amendment rather than treating it as a minor update, because the amendment
is what triggers any additional inspections the new category requires.
Simply adding new activity without amending the license is treated the
same as operating without a license for that activity.

Food trucks and mobile vendors follow a separate, shorter licensing track
managed jointly by Planning & Zoning and Public Works, since mobile
vendors also need approval for where they're allowed to park and operate.
Mobile vendor licenses are typically valid for six months and require
proof of a commissary kitchen for any vendor preparing food rather than
just reheating or assembling it on site.
